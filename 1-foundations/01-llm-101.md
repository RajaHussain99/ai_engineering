# Level 1: LLM 101

**Phase 1: Foundations** · Previous: [Level 0](00-software-engineering-fundamentals.md) · Next: [Level 2](02-working-with-llm-apis.md)

**Goal:** hold an accurate picture of what a large language model (LLM) does, so its
behaviour stops being surprising and you can pick a model for a task on evidence.

**Skip this level if** you can explain tokens, attention, sampling and context limits to a
colleague without looking anything up.

---

## 1. What a language model is

An LLM does one thing: given some text, it predicts what comes next. More exactly, it gives
every possible next piece of text a probability.

**Example.** Given "The capital of France is", the model might produce:

| Next piece | Probability |
|---|---|
| " Paris" | 92% |
| " a" | 3% |
| " the" | 1% |
| everything else | 4% |

It picks one (usually " Paris"), adds it to the text, and predicts again. A whole essay is
this one step repeated hundreds of times. Your phone's keyboard suggestions work on the
same idea, at a tiny fraction of the scale.

Two consequences follow, and they explain most of what you will see:

- **The model produces likely text, not checked facts.** It has no built-in step that
  looks anything up. When the likely continuation happens to be true, you get a correct
  answer. When it is not, you get a confident wrong one.
- **Everything is text continuation.** A "chat" is a document that looks like a
  conversation, which the model continues with the assistant's next turn.

---

## 2. Tokenization

Models do not read letters or whole words. They read **tokens**: chunks that are often a
word, part of a word, or punctuation.

**Example.** "Unbelievable results!" might be split into:

```
["Un", "believ", "able", " results", "!"]   → 5 tokens
```

A rough rule for English: 1 token is about three-quarters of a word, so 1,000 tokens is
about 750 words.

Why you care:

- **You pay per token**, for both what you send and what you get back.
- **Limits are in tokens.** A model's maximum input is a token count.
- **It explains odd failures.** Ask a model how many times "r" appears in "strawberry" and
  it may get it wrong, because it sees chunks like `["str", "aw", "berry"]`, not letters.
- **Languages differ.** The same sentence in Hindi or Japanese often uses more tokens than
  in English, so it costs more.

**Try it:**

```python
import tiktoken
enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
tokens = enc.encode("Unbelievable results!")
print(len(tokens), [enc.decode([t]) for t in tokens])
```

Each model family has its own tokenizer, so counts vary between models.

## 3. Inside the model

You do not need the maths to build products. You need the picture.

**Step 1: embeddings.** Each token is turned into a long list of numbers (a vector). You
can think of it as coordinates on a map of meaning. Tokens with related meanings sit close
together: "king" is near "queen", and far from "banana".

**Step 2: attention.** This is the key idea. For each token, the model looks at all the
other tokens and decides which ones matter for understanding it.

*Example:* "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because **it** was too big."
What does "it" refer to? Attention lets the model link "it" strongly to "trophy". Change
"big" to "small" and the link shifts to "suitcase".

**Step 3: repeat.** A transformer stacks dozens of layers of attention plus some
processing. Early layers pick up grammar; later layers pick up meaning and relationships.

**Step 4: predict.** The final layer turns the result into the probability table from
section 1.

The numbers inside those layers are called **parameters** or **weights**. A large model
has billions. They are what "the model" is, as a file.

---

## 4. How models learn

**Pre-training.** Start with random weights. Show the model a huge amount of text with the
next token hidden, let it guess, and nudge the weights slightly towards the right answer.
Repeat trillions of times. To get good at guessing, the model ends up absorbing grammar,
facts, reasoning patterns and writing styles.

**Fine-tuning for chat.** A freshly pre-trained model just continues text. Ask it a
question and it might reply with another question. A second round of training on example
conversations teaches it to act as a helpful assistant, and further training with human
feedback shapes its tone and what it declines to do.

**Training cutoff.** The model's knowledge stops at the date its training data ends.

*Example:* ask about an event from last month and the model either says it does not know
or, worse, invents an answer. Its own guess about today's date can be wrong too. The fix is
to supply current information in the prompt, which is what levels 4 and 5 are about.

---

## 5. How inference works

**Inference** means using a trained model to produce output.

- **Reading is fast, writing is slow.** The model reads your whole input at once, then
  writes its answer one token at a time. A 5,000-word input with a 50-word answer is quick.
  A 50-word input with a 5,000-word answer is slow.
- **Two speed measurements matter:**
  - *Time to first token:* how long before anything appears. This is what a chat user feels.
  - *Tokens per second:* how fast the rest arrives. This matters for long outputs.

*Example:* a chatbot that takes 0.5 seconds to start and then streams feels fast, even if
the full answer takes 8 seconds. A background job that summarises 10,000 documents does not
care about the first token; it cares about total time and cost.

- **Output tokens cost more** than input tokens, often several times more per token.
- **Repeated beginnings can be cached.** If many requests start with the same long
  instructions, providers can reuse the work and charge less. More in level 6.

## 6. Sampling

After the model produces its probability table, something has to pick a token. That choice
is called sampling, and you can tune it.

**Temperature** controls how adventurous the pick is.

*Example prompt:* "Suggest a name for a coffee shop."

| Temperature | Three runs might give |
|---|---|
| 0 | "The Daily Grind", "The Daily Grind", "The Daily Grind" |
| 1 | "Bean There", "The Steamy Cup", "Morning Ritual" |
| 1.5 | "Caffeine Cathedral", "Mug of the Moon", "Roastwick" |

- Low temperature: use for extraction, classification, code. You want the same answer every time.
- Higher temperature: use for brainstorming and creative writing.

Even at 0, results are not guaranteed to be identical between runs. Treat it as "mostly
consistent".

**Top-p** is another dial: only consider the smallest group of tokens whose probabilities
add up to p (say 90%), and ignore the long tail of unlikely ones. Adjust temperature or
top-p, not both. Some newer models fix these settings and do not let you change them.

**Max tokens** caps the length of the answer. If an answer stops mid-sentence, you probably
hit this limit.

**Stop sequences** are strings that end the output when they appear.

## 7. Reasoning models

Some models can "think" before answering: they write out private working, then give a
final answer. That extra working is called reasoning or extended thinking.

*Example:* "A bat and a ball cost $1.10. The bat costs $1 more than the ball. What does the
ball cost?" The quick, tempting answer is 10 cents. Working it through gives 5 cents.
Thinking time is what lets a model catch that.

The trade:

| | Without reasoning | With reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Fast | Slower, sometimes much slower |
| Cost | Lower | Higher (you pay for the thinking tokens) |
| Good for | Extraction, summaries, routing, simple questions | Maths, planning, tricky debugging, multi-step logic |

Rule of thumb: turn reasoning on when a careful person would need scratch paper.

## 8. The context window

The **context window** is everything the model can see for one response: your
instructions, the conversation so far, any documents you included, and the answer it is
writing. It has a maximum size in tokens.

**The model has no memory between requests.** This surprises almost everyone.

*Example:*

```
Request 1:  "My name is Priya."          → "Nice to meet you, Priya!"
Request 2:  "What's my name?"            → "I don't know your name."
```

Chat apps appear to remember because they quietly resend the whole conversation each time:

```
Request 2 (what the app actually sends):
  User: My name is Priya.
  Assistant: Nice to meet you, Priya!
  User: What's my name?
→ "Your name is Priya."
```

Consequences:

- Long conversations cost more with every turn, because the whole history is sent again.
- When the window fills up, something must be dropped or summarised.
- **Bigger is not always better.** With very long inputs, models recall details from the
  start and end better than from the middle. Put the important things where they will be noticed.

## 9. Capabilities and failure modes

**Good at:** writing and rewriting, summarising, translating, explaining, writing code,
pulling structured data out of messy text, classifying, brainstorming.

**Common failures, with causes:**

| Failure | Example | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucination | Cites a research paper that does not exist | Produces likely-looking text; nothing checks facts |
| Outdated knowledge | Says the newest phone is last year's | Training cutoff |
| Sycophancy | Agrees with your wrong claim after you push back | Trained to be agreeable |
| Arithmetic slips | Gets 4,817 × 392 slightly wrong | Predicts digits; does not calculate |
| Sensitivity to wording | Two phrasings of one question give different answers | Different text, different probabilities |
| Losing the thread | Forgets an instruction from 40 turns ago | Attention spread thin over long context |

Each one has a standard fix you will learn: give the model documents (level 4), give it a
calculator or code tool (level 2), write clearer instructions (level 3), manage long
conversations (level 6).

## 10. The model landscape, benchmarks and choosing

**Two splits to understand:**

- **Closed vs open-weight.** Closed models are reached only through the maker's service.
  Open-weight models can be downloaded and run on your own hardware.
- **Large vs small.** Large models are smarter, slower and dearer. Small ones are fast and
  cheap, and good enough for simple, high-volume work.

Most providers offer a family: a big model for hard problems, a mid-size one for everyday
work, and a small fast one.

**Benchmarks** are standard tests used to rank models. Use them to make a shortlist, not a
decision:

- Popular tests get "used up": once most models score above 90%, the test stops telling
  them apart.
- Test questions leak into training data, which inflates scores.
- A test of general knowledge says little about your invoices or your support tickets.

**How to actually choose:**

1. Collect 20 to 50 real examples of your task, with what a good answer looks like.
2. Run them through two or three candidate models.
3. Compare three things: quality, speed, and cost.
4. Pick the cheapest model that is good enough.

*Example:* sorting support emails into five categories. The small model gets 96% right at
a twentieth of the price of the large one, which gets 97%. The small model is the right
choice.
